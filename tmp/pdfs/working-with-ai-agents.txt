From language models to agents
A disclaimer: a fast-moving field


    ▶ AI capabilities, products, prices, and interfaces are changing extremely
      quickly.
    ▶ Today’s tools may age much faster than the ideas behind them.
    ▶ The more durable questions concern context, tools, planning, permissions,
      verification, evaluation, and reproducibility.
    ▶ Our goal is to understand those questions well enough to adapt.
    ▶ My opinion: AGI will not suddenly be here, but will diffuse slowly and
      those that understand what we’ll talk about today will experience it first.



                                                                                    4/69
From models to systems
   ▶ Earlier lectures focused on representation, prediction, inference, and how
     language models work.
   ▶ Today we move from studying a model output to studying a system that
     can act repeatedly on a research project.
   ▶ This raises a new set of questions: How should we organize, supervise, and
     evaluate such a system?
   ▶ This is the automate step in the course arc: measure → model → infer
     → automate.



             The model is only one part of the agentic system.

                                                                                  5/69
Why agents?


   ▶ Generative AI already makes code, search, synthesis, and drafting much
     cheaper.
   ▶ Agents add the ability to act inside an environment, rather than merely
     suggest what the researcher should do.
   ▶ They can inspect files, execute code, retrieve information, observe results,
     and revise their approach.
   ▶ This expands what can be delegated, as well as what must be supervised.




                                                                                    6/69
Chatbots, coding assistants, and agents

    ▶ With a chatbot, you provide context and receive a response.
    ▶ A coding assistant works inside the file or environment you are using.
    ▶ An agent can operate across a broader environment and choose
      intermediate actions.
    ▶ One useful shorthand is chat to repository: the AI moves into the project
      itself.



   An agent pursues an objective by taking actions, rather than producing
                              a single output.

                                                                                  7/69
The argument of this lecture

    ▶ Chat AI made generation cheap. Agentic AI increasingly makes multi-step
      execution cheap as well.
    ▶ Good prompts are not enough. Effective use also depends on context,
      planning, delegation, permissions, verification, and evals.
    ▶ Agents can help us think through decisions as well as carry them out.
    ▶ The researcher remains part of the process.



      Delegate work, use the agent to improve your thinking, and keep
                        responsibility for the result.

                                                                                8/69
What we need to remember about an LLM


   ▶ A useful simplification is that an LLM receives a finite sequence of tokens
     and generates further tokens.
   ▶ Training gives the model rich representations of language, facts, code,
     concepts, and patterns of reasoning.
   ▶ During inference, its immediate information comes from its parameters and
     the current context.
   ▶ A model on its own does not know your files, whether code ran successfully,
     or what happened in a previous session.

From model to agent: why a scaffold is needed

   ▶ Writing run analysis.py is not the same as executing it.
   ▶ A model on its own cannot inspect arbitrary files, run software, observe
     consequences, or maintain a long-lived project.
   ▶ The surrounding harness manages context, exposes tools, executes actions,
     records state, and calls the model again.
   ▶ Roughly speaking, an agent is a model inside a scaffold that lets it act
     repeatedly toward an objective.



    The model provides the capability. The scaffold lets it do the work.

Tools turn generation into action

    ▶ A chatbot can generate plausible R or Stata code.
    ▶ An agent can inspect existing code, modify it, execute it, observe the result,
      and revise.
    ▶ External feedback tells the system when its initial proposal has failed.
    ▶ This works especially well when success can be checked mechanically.




     Tools turn generation into action, then feed the result back to the
                                   model.

What does agency itself buy us?

    ▶ Galiani, López, and Sosa (2026) compare levels of agency on econometric
      and statistical coding tasks.
    ▶ Their chatbot writes code but cannot execute it; their agent can repeatedly
      execute and repair its work.
    ▶ Task success rises from roughly 74% to 96%.
    ▶ The task is already specified, so this is evidence about implementation,
      not about choosing a good empirical design.


  Source: Galiani, López, and Sosa (2026).

Agents are stochastic systems

    ▶ Repeated runs of the same task can produce different reasoning paths, tool
      calls, and outcomes.
    ▶ Early differences can compound because actions change the environment
      and later context.
    ▶ One successful run shows that the system can succeed, not that it usually
      will.
    ▶ We return later to what this implies for evaluation.



    Evaluate a stochastic system across repeated runs, not by anecdote.

                                                                                   16/69
Context is the agent’s working information

    ▶ On a particular call, context is what the model can access: instructions,
      history, relevant files, and tool outputs.
    ▶ Context is finite.
    ▶ Too little context forces guessing; too much irrelevant context can create
      distraction and drift.
    ▶ Using an agent well requires deliberate context management.




          More context is not always better. Choose it deliberately.


                                                                                   17/69
Project instructions: persistent context


    ▶ Project-level files such as AGENTS.md or CLAUDE.md provide standing
      context across sessions.
    ▶ These files can describe both operational conventions and research
      norms.
    ▶ Examples include project structure, commands, evidence standards, citation
      expectations, and decisions requiring human approval.
    ▶ Project instructions answer How does this project work? ; the current prompt
      answers What should happen now?

Building a reliable workflow with agents
Start simple


    ▶ You do not need multiple agents, custom skills, or external integrations to
      begin.
    ▶ A practical starting point is one agent, one research project, Git, and clear
      project instructions.
    ▶ Start by optimizing for inspectability rather than autonomy.
    ▶ Practice inspecting, planning, delegating, reviewing, and recovering.




                                                                                      20/69
Make the project legible to a new collaborator


    ▶ Structure the repository as if a smart new RA had joined today.
    ▶ That RA would need to understand the project, how it runs, what changed
      recently, and which rules govern the work.
    ▶ An agent needs the same information.
    ▶ Folder structure, README, recent commits, and project instructions give
      both a clear starting point.




                                                                                21/69
Minimum viable project setup


   ▶ Keep one project per folder; separate raw data, derived data, code, and
     generated output.
   ▶ Add a README.md, .gitignore, and standing project instructions.
   ▶ Use a reproducible software environment where appropriate (renv, venv,
     conda, etc.).
   ▶ Keep restricted data and credentials out of version control.
   ▶ A reproducible environment isolates dependencies, but it is not a security
     sandbox.

Git: the minimum you need
   ▶ A repository tracks project history; a commit is a snapshot; a diff shows
     exactly what changed.
   ▶ Minimal useful commands: git init, git add, git commit, git log,
     and git restore.
   ▶ Using a GUI such as GitHub Desktop is fine.
   ▶ Agents increase the return to version control because they can modify many
     files very quickly.



  Git matters because an agent can change more code in one minute than
                     you can comfortably remember.

Planning is part of the work
    ▶ Many conventional ML tasks have explicit targets and metrics; open-ended
      research problems often do not.
    ▶ Before delegating execution, the researcher and agent often need to define
      the task together.
    ▶ Planning brings ambiguities, alternatives, substantive choices, and possible
      failure points.
    ▶ Do not simply accept the agent’s first plan. Improve it through dialogue.



     Planning makes an open-ended research problem precise enough to
                               delegate.

                                                                                     27/69
A disciplined agent workflow

    ▶ Explore the project and relevant context.
    ▶ Plan and challenge alternative approaches.
    ▶ Agree on deliverables, constraints, checkpoints, and escalation conditions.
    ▶ Execute bounded pieces, then inspect and update.
    ▶ Commit only after you understand the resulting project state.




        Explore → plan → challenge → execute → inspect → update


                                                                                    28/69
Develop the plan together

    ▶ Tell me what you think I am trying to accomplish, and what remains ambiguous.

    ▶ Give me two or three plausible approaches and explain their trade-offs.

    ▶ Which choices here are substantive research decisions?

    ▶ What could make this plan fail, and what intermediate evidence would tell us
      early?

             Now synthesize our discussion into a plan. Do not execute yet.




    Work through the plan with the agent instead of accepting its first version.

                                                                                      29/69
A good plan is a research artifact

    ▶ For substantial work, save the plan in project notes or PLAN.md.
    ▶ Record the objective, decisions, proposed steps, constraints, checkpoints,
      and unresolved questions.
    ▶ A written plan preserves context across sessions and makes human decisions
      explicit.
    ▶ It also reduces what the agent has to guess.




         A written plan builds understanding before execution starts.


                                                                                   30/69
Verifiability determines task size

    ▶ Before execution, state what successful output should look like.
    ▶ Identify two or three checks you will run afterwards.
    ▶ If you cannot say how you would know the task succeeded, it is probably too
      broad.
    ▶ Break it down until you can inspect meaningful intermediate outputs.




             If you cannot verify the whole task, make it smaller.


                                                                                    31/69
What makes a task suitable for delegation?

    1. Can I specify the task clearly?
    2. Can I tell whether it succeeded?
    3. How much domain judgment is required to recognize a sensible
       result?

    ▶ Delegation is easier when objectives and success criteria are explicit.
    ▶ Tasks that rely heavily on tacit institutional knowledge or research taste are
      harder to delegate.



  Delegate tasks when you can state the objective and the success criteria.

                                                                                       32/69
Skills: reusable research procedures


    ▶ A skill stores instructions for a recurring class of tasks.
    ▶ It can include scripts, templates, references, or checklists.
    ▶ Examples: replication audit, citation verification, code review, and literature
      evidence extraction.
    ▶ If you keep giving essentially the same instructions, consider turning them
      into a procedure.




                                                                                        33/69
MCP: connecting agents to external systems

   ▶ Model Context Protocol (MCP) provides a standardized way to connect
     an agent application to external systems.
   ▶ An MCP server can expose actions (tools) and external information
     (resources).
   ▶ For example, an agent can connect to reference managers, literature
     databases, institutional databases, and data warehouses.
   ▶ Each integration expands the permission and security surface.



      Use MCP when the task requires a specific external capability.

                                                                           34/69
Subagents: additional workers

    ▶ Some systems can create separate agent instances with their own contexts.
    ▶ Separate agents make sense when subtasks genuinely parallelize or benefit
      from separation.
    ▶ For example, you might run parallel literature searches or pair an
      implementation agent with an audit agent.
    ▶ Adding more agents makes coordination, duplicated work, cost, and
      provenance harder to manage.



   Add agents only when the structure of the task gives them a clear role.

   ▶ Agents can help brainstorm mechanisms, expose assumptions, connect

   ▶ They can derive competing predictions and simulate skeptical seminar

Example: improving a research plan
  Puzzle: After a minimum-wage increase, wages rise but employment changes
  little.
     ▶ Start from one preferred explanation, such as wage-setting power.
     ▶ Ask the agent to propose serious competing mechanisms and predictions
        that distinguish them.
     ▶ Then ask which predictions can be tested cheaply with the available data.
     ▶ Compare alternatives such as hours, turnover, and price adjustment; then
        decide which empirical path deserves attention.



  Use the agent to consider more possibilities. Then decide what deserves
                                attention.
                                                                                   44/69
Brainstorming without outsourcing your thinking

    ▶ Write down your current view before prompting.
    ▶ Ask for competing mechanisms, counterarguments, or discriminating
      predictions.
    ▶ The agent can play a skeptical referee, seminar participant, Socratic
      questioner, and alternative-theory generator.
    ▶ Ask explicitly for disagreement rather than reinforcement.



   Start with your own view, let AI challenge it, then evaluate the result
                                  yourself.

From repeated prompts to reusable procedures


   ▶ The same research tasks often recur, including citation checking, replication
     audits, and referee simulations.
   ▶ Write the procedure down and create Skill files so the agent can apply it
     consistently.
   ▶ Doing so also forces the researcher to make tacit standards explicit.
   ▶ The resulting Skill files can then be version-controlled, evaluated, and

Verification, evaluation, and research integrity
The central risk: comprehension debt

    ▶ Agents can generate work faster than researchers can carefully inspect it.
    ▶ A project can accumulate consequential decisions that its author no longer
      understands.
    ▶ Comprehension debt is the liability created by accepting work without
      understanding it.
    ▶ If a result enters the paper, the researcher should be able to explain how it
      was produced and why.



                 Speed only helps if understanding keeps pace.

Verify incrementally

    ▶ When AI generates a variable, validation can focus on that variable. With
      agents, the generated object may be an entire workflow.
    ▶ Verification begins before execution by defining expected checkpoints.
    ▶ During execution, inspect coherent increments rather than waiting for the
      final artifact.
    ▶ Compare outputs, code changes, sample counts, and logs against the plan.



      A generated workflow creates more to validate than a generated
                                 variable.

                                                                                  50/69
Common failure modes


   ▶ Hallucination: plausible but nonexistent sources or claims.
   ▶ Plausible-but-wrong implementation: code runs, but the analysis is
     wrong.
   ▶ Sycophancy: the agent reinforces your preferred interpretation.
   ▶ Context degradation / task drift: long-running work loses the objective.
   ▶ Resource drift: autonomous work consumes time or tokens without enough
     value in return.




                                                                                51/69
Instructions are not constraints

    ▶ Natural-language instructions influence behavior but do not guarantee
      compliance.
    ▶ Instruction: Never modify raw data.
    ▶ Verification: Check afterwards whether raw data changed.
    ▶ Constraint: Make writes to data/raw/ technically unavailable.
    ▶ When the consequences are stronger, enforcement should be stronger too.




      Do not rely on instructions alone to protect important invariants.


                                                                                52/69
Data access, permissions, and security


    ▶ Know what the agent can read, modify, execute, upload, and access
      externally.
    ▶ Pay particular attention to confidential data, credentials, IRB/data-use
      restrictions, and internet access.
    ▶ Start with narrow permissions and expand deliberately.
    ▶ Do not assume that work done inside your project stays on your machine.




                                                                                 53/69
Disclosure, authorship, and professional norms


    ▶ Rules for substantive AI use are still evolving.
    ▶ Keep a simple record of meaningful AI use during the project.
    ▶ Check institutional, journal, funder, and data-owner rules again before
      submission.
    ▶ Authors remain accountable for claims, citations, analysis, and reported
      numbers, whatever tools they used.

You already know why we evaluate models

   ▶ In predictive ML, we define a metric and evaluate performance on cases not
     used to fit or tune the model.
   ▶ Earlier lectures covered train/validation/test splits, cross-validation, leakage,
     and validation overfitting.
   ▶ Agentic systems raise a similar evaluation problem at another level.
   ▶ We now evaluate the model + instructions + tools + scaffold +

                                                                                         55/69
Verification and evals are different



    ▶ Verification: Is this particular artifact correct?
    ▶ Evaluation: How reliably does this agent setup perform a recurring class of
      tasks?
    ▶ Verification might check an estimate, citation, sample, or code change.
    ▶ An eval compares performance across repeated representative tasks.




                                                                                    56/69
One successful run does not establish reliability

    ▶ Because agent behavior is stochastic, each task has a success probability.
    ▶ A trial is one attempt; a grader determines whether it succeeded.
    ▶ It can do this may mean one successful run exists.
    ▶ I can rely on this requires consistently good performance across
      representative tasks.




    Capability tells us whether success is possible. Reliability tells us how
                              often to expect it.

A short detour: from AlphaGo to AlphaGo Zero
    ▶ Go is a board game with simple rules but an enormous number of possible
      positions. A computer cannot examine every continuation.
    ▶ AlphaGo (2016) first learned from games played by human experts, improved
      through self-play and reinforcement learning, and used tree search to compare
      possible moves. It defeated Lee Sedol, one of the world’s strongest players.
    ▶ AlphaGo Zero (2017) was given only the rules. It learned by playing against
      itself and soon surpassed the earlier version.
  Why was this possible? Every game generated new experience and ended with an
  unambiguous signal: win or lose.
  An analogy: reasoning models follow a similar path: imitate human data → spend
  more computation at inference → use reinforcement learning beyond imitation.
  The lesson: When learning, search, and feedback can all scale, a system may move
  beyond human examples.
  Source: Google DeepMind, AlphaGo and AlphaGo Zero; Sutton (2019); Brown (2026).     61/69

When execution gets cheaper, what becomes scarce?

   ▶ Agents make coding, search, data processing, robustness, drafting, and
     documentation cheaper.
   ▶ Research still needs complementary inputs.
   ▶ Bottlenecks may shift toward questions and judgment, relationships
     and access, and verification capacity.
   ▶ Trust may matter more when convincing-looking research is cheaper to
     produce.



    As execution gets cheaper, another input becomes the bottleneck.

                                                                              64/69
AI may raise the floor and therefore the bar

    ▶ If everyone can cheaply produce clean code, polished prose, extensive
      robustness, and professional figures, these dimensions become less scarce.
    ▶ Papers may be expected to deliver more on question quality, data,
      identification, novelty, and interpretation.
    ▶ AI also makes it cheaper to generate candidate ideas.
    ▶ More candidate ideas increase the value of selection and research taste.



       AI may raise the production floor while raising the standard for
                               contribution.

How do you build judgment and taste?
  Turns out, not much may have changed for PhD students:
   ▶ Learn a literature deeply enough to understand why its frontier is where it is.
   ▶ Read papers as sequences of research decisions, not only as results.
   ▶ Attend seminars, discuss immature ideas, and learn from failed attempts.
   ▶ Build institutional knowledge, relationships, and exposure to the setting.
   ▶ Use AI to speed up the apprenticeship: quiz yourself, simulate objections,
      and generate alternatives. It should not replace your own evaluation.



   Delegate work, use AI to improve your thinking, and keep responsibility
                               for the result.
